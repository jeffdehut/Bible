# The Open Plain English Translation

A new translation of the Bible into plain English, made directly from the Hebrew, Aramaic and
Greek texts in this repository. It aims to say what the original says, as clearly as everyday
English allows, and to show its work wherever a choice had to be made. It is open: anyone may
use, copy, adapt and redistribute it.

**Status:** first drafts. The whole Old Testament (Genesis to Malachi) and the Gospel of Mark
are drafted, with John 1 (946 of 1,189 chapters). See [translation/STATUS.md](translation/STATUS.md). No chapter has had human review
yet, so nothing here should be quoted as a finished translation.

## Goals

1. **Accurate meaning.** Every verse says what the source text says. Nothing is added, nothing
   is left out, and nothing is softened or sharpened.
2. **Plain English.** Natural modern English that a reader around 12–14 years old can follow.
   Sentences are short, vocabulary is everyday, and idioms are rendered by their meaning.
3. **Transparency.** Where the source can reasonably be read more than one way, where an
   idiom or image has been restated, or where manuscripts differ in a way that matters, a
   note says so. A reader should always be able to find out what the original says.

## Principles

**Translate the source as it stands.** The Old Testament follows the Leningrad Codex (UXLC
2.6) and the New Testament the SBL Greek New Testament (v1.2), the same texts verified in
this repository. Where the Hebrew vowel points (added in the Middle Ages) read awkwardly and
the consonants support a more natural reading, we may follow that reading and give the
pointed reading in a note. Significant differences from other editions, such as those in
the SBLGNT apparatus or the Septuagint, are noted rather than adopted.

**Meaning-based, not a paraphrase.** We translate thought for thought within each verse, not
word for word. We keep:
- the imagery where an English reader can follow it ("Yahweh is my shepherd")
- the repetition and structure of poetry and narrative
- the text's own ambiguity where it is deliberate or unresolved

Where the plain rendering states a meaning the words only imply, the literal wording goes in
a note.

**Notes on manuscripts cite our sources.** Variant readings in the New Testament are noted from
the SBLGNT apparatus; in the Old Testament, from the ketiv/qere and notes in UXLC and the
ancient versions where widely documented. A note never claims manuscript evidence the
sources here do not record.

**No interpretation beyond the text.** We don't harmonize passages, resolve doctrinal
questions, or add explanatory words to the text itself. Explanations go in notes.

**Consistency.** Key terms are translated the same way throughout unless context requires
otherwise, and each such case is noted. The glossary below records the decisions.

**Cross-references in notes** use the same numbering as this repository's texts: Hebrew numbering in the Old Testament, with the English number added where it differs.

**Verse numbers follow the source.** In the Old Testament this is the Hebrew numbering, so
Psalm titles are verse 1 and some chapters are divided differently from English Bibles. The
connection notes map between the two.

## Translator's checklist

These rules come from drafting the Gospel of Mark. Every chapter follows them.

**Reading the source.** `python3 scripts/source.py <book> <chapter>` prints the chapter from
`text/hebrew` or `text/greek`. For the New Testament it adds the SBLGNT apparatus. For the Old
Testament it spells out each ketiv/qere and shows the English verse number wherever it differs
from the Hebrew.

**Text.**
- Translate every verse, and only what is there. Don't add words to make a point clearer: if a
  subject or object has to be supplied, use the one the context gives and keep it minimal
  (Mark 10:6 says "he made them", with "God" in a note). Where a Hebrew clause has no verb and
  English needs one (Nehemiah 3:25), supply the plainest verb the context implies and note it.
  This is for narrative and lists. The ordinary Hebrew clause without a verb ("Yahweh my
  shepherd") simply takes "is" or "are" in English, with no note.
- Narrative is in the past tense, including Greek and Hebrew historic presents. Mark's εὐθύς is
  "at once" or "immediately", not always "and immediately".
- American spelling: "honor", "neighbor", "forever", "worshiped", "judgment".
- Poetry keeps its parallel lines and its images. Each verse is still one line in the file.
- Divine name: every יהוה is "Yahweh", whatever its vowels (יְהוִה too). Theophoric
  names (Jonathan, Elijah) are spelled as in most English Bibles.
- Hebrew paragraph marks (פ, ס) at the end of a verse and UXLC's transcription marks (`[t]`,
  `[c]`, `[m]`, `[d]`, `[q]`, `[y]`) are not text. Don't translate them or note them. `[X]`
  marks a verse missing from the Leningrad Codex (Joshua 21:36–37): translate it and say so
  in a note.
- Ketiv/qere: translate the qere (the read form). Where the ketiv (the written form) would be
  translated differently in English, even only in number ("its post"/"its posts"), give it in a
  note; where the English would be the same, don't. Where many in one chapter differ only in
  singular and plural (Ezekiel 40), one combined note is enough:
  "The Hebrew text is written 'X' but read 'Y'." Differences of spelling alone are not noted.
- A speech that runs on into the next chapter reopens its quotation at the chapter's first
  verse, as English does for a new paragraph of the same speech, and closes it only where the
  speech ends.
  Long law codes (Exodus 21–23, Leviticus, Deuteronomy 12–26) may instead be set without
  quotation marks, with a note at the start saying who is speaking and where the speech ends.
- Numbers: words for one to one hundred; for whole hundreds up to nine hundred ("four
  hundred"); for one to one hundred thousands ("a thousand", "twelve thousand", "twenty-four
  thousand", "a hundred thousand"); and for whole hundreds of thousands ("six hundred
  thousand"). Digits for all others, with commas from 1,000 ("130 years", "250", "1,100",
  "1,775 shekels", "46,500", "120,000"). Never "eleven hundred" or "seventeen hundred". In a census, list, battle tally or calculation where some
  numbers need digits, all of its numbers are digits (Numbers 1, Judges 20, Ezra 2). Numbers
  counted together in one sentence form such a list: "1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen". In a
  passage of measurements (Exodus 26, Ezekiel 40–48) every measurement is in digits, while counts
  of other things ("three gates") follow the general rule. Where the Hebrew gives a measurement
  without its unit (Ezekiel 45:1), don't add the unit in the text; say in a note what is meant. Ordinals in words ("the twenty-seventh day").
  Modern measurements and dates in notes are digits.
- Scribal marks: the extraordinary points (`[4]`), the inverted nuns (`[8]`) and the unusual
  size or position of large, small or suspended letters (`[5]`–`[7]`) are not reproduced; the
  letters themselves are translated as part of their word (Judges 18:30 "Manasseh", with
  "Moses" in a note). A short note mentions each mark where it occurs ("In the Hebrew text, dots are written over this word, a scribal mark of doubt").
- Where Aramaic begins and ends (Ezra 4:8–6:18, 7:12–26; Jeremiah 10:11; Daniel 2:4b–7:28),
  say so in a note.
- Psalm title terms: a term whose meaning is known is translated ("For the director", "A song of
  ascents", a tune name such as "The Doe of the Dawn" in quotation marks); one whose meaning is
  unknown is transliterated with the Hebrew in a note ("According to Alamoth"). Genre words
  stay lowercase: maskil, miktam, shiggaion. *Selah* stays where the Hebrew has it, even
  mid-sentence.
- Where the Septuagint gives a title to a psalm that has none in the Hebrew, a short note may
  give it; it is not required.
- English verse numbers in notes are written "(89:52 in most English Bibles)".
- Psalm titles are part of verse 1 (or verses 1–2) in the Hebrew. Where that makes this
  psalm's numbering differ from English Bibles, verse 1 has a note saying so. Elsewhere, the
  first verse whose number differs from English Bibles gets a note ("Verses 1–5 are 4:21–25
  in most English Bibles").

**Notes.**
- Number notes `[^1]`, `[^2]` … in the order they first appear. Put the marker after the word
  or phrase it explains, after any punctuation that ends the phrase.
- Standard forms: `Literally "…".` `Or "…".` `Hebrew *nephesh*, …` `Greek *logos*, …`
  Transliterate simply: *chesed*, *ruach*, *nephesh*, *lestes*.
- Old Testament quotations and allusions get a reference in a note (Mark 12:10–11). If the
  quotation names Yahweh, the note says: `In the Hebrew, "the Lord" is God's personal name,
  Yahweh.` If a New Testament quotation follows the Greek Septuagint where it differs from the
  Hebrew, say so.
- The Yahweh note, at the first occurrence in each chapter: `*Yahweh*: God's personal name
  (Hebrew YHWH), traditionally rendered "the LORD" in English Bibles.`
- **New Testament variants** come only from the SBLGNT apparatus. Note those that change the
  meaning: a word, phrase or verse added or left out, or a different word. Spelling, word
  order, the article, εὐθύς/εὐθέως and similar differences are not noted. Wording:
  - a reading supported by RP, the Byzantine majority text (alone or with others): "Many
    manuscripts add/read …"
  - a reading of WH, Treg or NA28 without RP (NA28 alone too, as "Jesus Barabbas" at Matthew
    27:16): "Some manuscripts read …"
  - where the SBLGNT follows a minority (its reading is marked only by Holmes, or by RP
    alone): "These words are in the SBLGNT, following …; many modern editions leave them out."
  - a verse that is absent: the verse number is skipped in the file, and the previous verse
    has a note giving the verse and its words: "Many manuscripts add verse 26: '…'. The SBLGNT
    and other critical editions leave it out, so this translation has no verse 26."
  - a conjecture printed by an edition (Acts 16:12 in NA28): "Some editions read, by a
    conjectural change, …"; where the SBLGNT follows one edition against the others, name it:
    "The SBLGNT here follows Westcott and Hort; other editions read …".
  - The apparatus records editions, not manuscripts, so notes don't claim more ("the earliest
    manuscripts"). The exception is the few famous passages whose manuscript evidence is well
    documented (Mark 16:8–20, John 7:53–8:11, John 1:18), where the major witnesses may be named.
  Check the single-word variants too: one word can change the meaning (Mark 1:41).
  - Text the SBLGNT prints in double brackets ⟦ ⟧ (Mark 16:9–20, John 7:53–8:11) keeps them in
    the translation, with a note on the manuscript evidence, as in Mark 16.
  - A few famous readings are found only in the Textus Receptus, the basis of the King James
    Version, and not in the apparatus (1 John 5:7–8, Acts 8:37). They may be noted as "The
    Textus Receptus (followed by the King James Version) adds …", after checking the words in
    this repository's `text/greek-tr/`.
- **Old Testament variants**: the ketiv/qere, and well-known, well-documented readings of
  the Septuagint (including 1 Esdras, its parallel to Ezra–Nehemiah), the Dead Sea Scrolls, the
  Samaritan Pentateuch, the Syriac, the Aramaic Targums or the Vulgate
  (for example Deuteronomy 32:8, Psalm 22:17). Name the version: "The Septuagint reads …".
  A well-documented reading of medieval Hebrew manuscripts may be cited as "Some Hebrew
  manuscripts read …". Only note a reading you are sure of. If in doubt, leave it out. Never
  say just "some manuscripts" in the Old Testament. Every note citing an ancient version is
  verified against a critical apparatus (BHS/BHQ, Göttingen or Rahlfs Septuagint) at the
  *checked* stage.
- Conjectural emendations (changes to the Hebrew proposed by scholars, with no manuscript
  behind them) may be given only where the Hebrew is very difficult, and always worded so they
  can't be mistaken for manuscript evidence: "Some scholars read *gal*, 'a heap', with a small
  change to the Hebrew." A reading that keeps the consonants and changes only the vowels is
  given as "The same consonants can be read …"; one that divides the same letters into words
  differently is treated the same way. Where the pointed text is very awkward and such a
  reading is clearly better, the text may follow it, with the pointed reading in a note. The
  rabbinic "corrections of the scribes" (*tiqqune sopherim*) may be cited by that name.
- In the Aramaic chapters, a ketiv/qere note says "The Aramaic text is written … but read …". A ketiv that differs only in person or number, with no change to the
  English, is not noted.
- A ketiv/qere that changes the form of a name (Janim/Janum) is noted; one that only changes
  word order is noted only if the English would differ. Where the Septuagint arranges a whole
  passage differently (as in 1 Kings 20–21, Jeremiah, Proverbs 24–31), one note at the start of
  the passage says so.
- In prophetic and poetic books, Yahweh's own words in the poems are not put in quotation marks
  (as in Deuteronomy 32 and Isaiah); a song or saying the text introduces as speech ("On that
  day you will say: …") is, and so is a dialogue in which speakers alternate (Isaiah 63:1–6).
  Words the prophet reports others saying (Malachi's "But you ask, 'How …?'") are quoted; a
  speech a poem only imagines, with no speaker named, is not.
  A document given whole without an introduction (Nebuchadnezzar's letter, Daniel 3:31–4:34)
  takes no quotation marks.
- Quotation marks: double for speech, single for a quotation inside it, and so on alternating
  for deeper levels. The messenger formula ("This is what Yahweh says: …") inside a message
  takes a colon and no marks of its own (Jeremiah 27:2–11).
- Yahweh's speech in a prose narrative is quoted like anyone's ("Yahweh said to Jeremiah,
  '…'"). A whole oracle or sermon in Yahweh's voice is not put in quotation marks.
- Within a chapter, a speech's quotation is opened once and closed once; it isn't reopened at
  each verse. A "list" for the numbers rule is the paragraph its numbers belong to.
- Technical terms (tabernacle furnishings, sacrifices, priestly garments) get a full note at
  their first occurrence in a book. Later chapters give a short note only where a reader needs
  it, pointing back: "See 25:17."
- Notes explain, they don't preach: meaning, alternatives, background, references. No
  devotional or doctrinal commentary.

**The New Testament and the Old.**
- Where Matthew and Luke share a passage with Mark and the Greek is the same, use the same
  English as `translation/41-mark/`. The Gospels may differ only where the Greek does.
- An Old Testament quotation follows this translation's Old Testament (`translation/`) where
  the Greek renders the Hebrew. Where the New Testament follows the Septuagint and it differs
  from the Hebrew, translate the Greek as it stands and say so in a note (Hebrews 10:5).
- The note on an Old Testament quotation gives the reference in Hebrew numbering with the
  English added: "Psalm 2:7"; "Joel 3:1–5 (2:28–32 in most English Bibles)".

**Before you finish.** `python3 scripts/translation.py --check <book>` must pass.

## Format

Each chapter is one file, `translation/NN-book/CCC.md` (e.g. `translation/43-john/001.md`):

```markdown
---
book: John
chapter: 1
source: SBLGNT v1.2
status: draft          # draft → checked → reviewed → approved
translator: Claude (AI), pilot
reviewers: []
---

**1** In the beginning was the Word. The Word was with God, and the Word was God.[^1]

[^1]: *Word*: Greek *logos* ...
```

- One `**N** text` line for every verse in the source chapter, and nothing else.
- Notes are Markdown footnotes, numbered within the chapter.
- `python3 scripts/translation.py` checks every chapter against its source and fails on any
  missing or extra verse, or on unused or undefined notes. It then compiles the chapters into
  `text/english/NN-book.md` and writes `translation/STATUS.md`. The chapter notes in
  `connections/` show the translation beside the original.

## Process and review

1. **Draft.** Translated from the Hebrew or Greek of this repository, verse by verse, with
   notes. Pilot drafts are AI-generated (Claude), and every file's `translator` field says so.
2. **Checked.** Automated checks pass, and the chapter is compared against the source and a
   lexicon for omissions and additions.
3. **Reviewed.** At least one reviewer who reads the source language confirms every verse,
   and their names are recorded in `reviewers`.
4. **Approved.** Accepted for the published translation.

The published text (`text/english/`) shows each chapter's status, so readers know what
they're reading.
