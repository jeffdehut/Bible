**1** For the director. A Maskil of the sons of Korah.[^1]

**2** As a deer[^2] longs for streams of water, so my soul[^3] longs for you, God.[^4]

**3** My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When may I come and appear before God?[^5]

**4** My tears have been my food day and night, while people say to me all day long, "Where is your God?"

**5** These things I remember as I pour out my soul:[^6] how I used to go along with the throng,[^7] leading them in procession[^8] to the house of God, with shouts of joy and thanksgiving, a crowd keeping festival.

**6** Why are you downcast, my soul? Why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God, for I will yet praise him for the saving help of his presence.[^9]

**7** My God, my soul is downcast within me; so I remember you from the land of the Jordan and the heights of Hermon,[^10] from Mount Mizar.

**8** Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls;[^11] all your breakers and your waves have swept over me.

**9** By day Yahweh[^12] commands his faithful love,[^13] and at night his song[^14] is with me, a prayer to the God of my life.

**10** I say to God, my rock, "Why have you forgotten me? Why must I go about in mourning,[^15] oppressed by the enemy?"

**11** As if my bones were being crushed,[^16] my adversaries taunt me, saying to me all day long, "Where is your God?"

**12** Why are you downcast, my soul? Why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God, for I will yet praise him, my saving help[^17] and my God.

[^1]: *For the director*: Hebrew *lamenatseach*, probably the leader of the temple music. *Maskil*: a term of uncertain meaning, perhaps "a skillful psalm" or "a psalm of instruction". *Of the sons of Korah*: or "for the sons of Korah", a family of Levites who served as temple singers (2 Chronicles 20:19). In the Hebrew text the title is verse 1, so verses 2–12 are 42:1–11 in most English Bibles. Psalms 42 and 43 were probably once a single psalm: they share a refrain (42:6, 12; 43:5), Psalm 43 has no title of its own, and many Hebrew manuscripts write them as one.
[^2]: The noun is masculine but the verb is feminine, so perhaps "a doe".
[^3]: Hebrew *nephesh*: soul, life, self. Also in verses 3, 5, 6, 7 and 12.
[^4]: Psalms 42–83 are often called the "Elohistic Psalter". In them God is mostly called *Elohim*, "God", where other psalms use his personal name, Yahweh. Compare Psalm 53 with Psalm 14, and Psalm 70 with 40:14–18 (English 40:13–17), where the same lines appear with *Elohim* in place of Yahweh. This translation renders each name as it stands.
[^5]: The vowel points read "appear before God", the usual phrase for coming to worship at the sanctuary (Exodus 23:15; 34:23). The same consonants can be read "see the face of God".
[^6]: Literally "I remember these things, and I pour out my soul upon me", that is, in grief.
[^7]: Hebrew *sakh*, found only here, so the meaning is uncertain.
[^8]: Hebrew *eddaddem*, an uncertain form, perhaps "I walked slowly with them".
[^9]: Literally "the salvations of his face". In verse 12 and 43:5 the refrain ends "the salvation of my face and my God". The Septuagint has that wording here too, joining the first word of verse 7, "my God", to this verse.
[^10]: Literally "the Hermons", the peaks of Mount Hermon near the sources of the Jordan in the far north. *Mizar* means "small"; the mountain is unknown.
[^11]: Or "channels", "torrents". The word appears elsewhere only in 2 Samuel 5:8.
[^12]: *Yahweh*: God's personal name (Hebrew YHWH), traditionally rendered "the LORD" in English Bibles.
[^13]: Hebrew *chesed*: loyal, faithful love.
[^14]: The Hebrew text is written "a song" but read "his song".
[^15]: Literally "dark", wearing the dark clothes of mourning.
[^16]: Literally "With a shattering in my bones".
[^17]: Literally "the salvation of my face".
