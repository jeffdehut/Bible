**1** A song of ascents.[^1] To the LORD[^2] I called in my distress, and he answered me.

**2** LORD, rescue me[^3] from lying lips, from a deceitful tongue.

**3** What will he give you, and what more will he do to you,[^4] you deceitful tongue?

**4** A warrior's sharp arrows, with burning coals of the broom tree![^5]

**5** Woe is me, that I live as a stranger in Meshech, that I dwell among the tents of Kedar![^6]

**6** Too long I have lived[^7] with those who hate peace.

**7** I am for peace,[^8] but when I speak, they are for war.

[^1]: Hebrew *shir ha-ma'alot*, "a song of the goings up". Psalms 120–134 all have this title. They were probably sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for the festivals; Jewish tradition also linked them with the fifteen steps in the temple courts where the Levites sang. In the Hebrew text the title is part of verse 1. In this psalm it shares the verse with the first line, so the verse numbers match English Bibles.
[^2]: *LORD*: God's personal name, Hebrew YHWH, written "the LORD" in capitals as in most English Bibles.
[^3]: Literally "rescue my soul (*nephesh*)".
[^4]: The words echo an oath, "May God do so to you, and more also" (1 Samuel 3:17): what punishment will God give the liar?
[^5]: The wood of the broom tree makes charcoal that burns long and hot. The liar's punishment will fit the tongue's sharp, burning words.
[^6]: Meshech was a people of the far north, near the Black Sea (Genesis 10:2; Ezekiel 38:2), and Kedar a tribe of the Arabian desert (Genesis 25:13; Isaiah 21:16–17). The two names, far apart, probably stand for hostile, warlike neighbors in general.
[^7]: Literally "My soul (*nephesh*) has dwelt for itself too long".
[^8]: Literally "I (am) peace".
