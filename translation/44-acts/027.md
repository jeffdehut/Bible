**1** When it was decided that we[^1] would sail for Italy, they handed Paul and some other prisoners over to a centurion[^2] named Julius, of the Augustan Cohort.[^3]

**2** We boarded a ship from Adramyttium[^4] that was about to sail to the ports along the coast of Asia, and we put out to sea. Aristarchus, a Macedonian from Thessalonica, was with us.

**3** The next day we put in at Sidon. Julius treated Paul kindly and allowed him to go to his friends and be cared for.

**4** From there we put out to sea and sailed under the lee of Cyprus,[^5] because the winds were against us.

**5** We sailed across the open sea off Cilicia and Pamphylia and came to Myra in Lycia.

**6** There the centurion found an Alexandrian ship[^6] sailing for Italy and put us on board.

**7** For a good many days we made slow headway, and with difficulty we arrived off Cnidus. Since the wind would not let us go any farther,[^7] we sailed under the lee of Crete, off Salmone.[^8]

**8** Sailing along the coast with difficulty, we came to a place called Fair Havens, near the town of Lasea.

**9** Much time had now passed, and sailing had become dangerous, because by now even the Fast[^9] was over. So Paul gave them this advice:

**10** "Men, I can see that this voyage is going to end in disaster and heavy loss, not only of the cargo and the ship but also of our lives."

**11** But the centurion paid more attention to the pilot and to the owner of the ship[^10] than to what Paul said.

**12** Since the harbor was not suitable to spend the winter in, the majority decided to put out to sea from there, in the hope that they could somehow reach Phoenix and spend the winter there. Phoenix is a harbor of Crete, facing southwest and northwest.[^11]

**13** When a gentle south wind began to blow, they thought they had got what they wanted. So they weighed anchor and sailed along Crete, close to the shore.[^12]

**14** But before long a wind of hurricane force, called the Northeaster,[^13] rushed down from the island.[^14]

**15** The ship was caught by it and could not head into the wind,[^15] so we gave way to it and let ourselves be driven along.

**16** As we ran under the lee of a small island called Cauda,[^16] we were barely able to get the ship's boat[^17] under control.

**17** After the sailors had hoisted it aboard, they used supports to undergird the ship.[^18] They were afraid that they would run aground on the Syrtis,[^19] so they lowered the sea anchor[^20] and in this way were driven along.

**18** We were being battered so violently by the storm that the next day they began to throw the cargo overboard,[^21]

**19** and on the third day they threw the ship's gear[^22] overboard with their own hands.[^23]

**20** For many days neither sun nor stars appeared, and the storm continued to rage. At last all hope of our being saved was slipping away.

**21** They had gone a long time without food. Then Paul stood up among them and said, "Men, you should have listened to me and not set sail from Crete. You would have been spared this disaster and loss.[^24]

**22** But now I urge you to take courage, for not one of you will lose his life; only the ship will be lost.

**23** For last night an angel of the God to whom I belong and whom I worship stood beside me

**24** and said, 'Do not be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar, and God has granted you the lives of all who are sailing with you.'

**25** So take courage, men, for I trust God that it will turn out just as I have been told.

**26** But we must run aground on some island."

**27** On the fourteenth night, as we were being driven across the Adriatic Sea,[^25] about midnight the sailors suspected that they were nearing land.

**28** They took soundings and found that the water was twenty fathoms deep. A little farther on they took soundings again and found it was fifteen fathoms.[^26]

**29** Fearing that we might run aground somewhere on the rocks, they dropped four anchors from the stern and prayed for daylight to come.

**30** The sailors tried to escape from the ship. They lowered the boat into the sea, pretending that they were going to run out anchors from the bow.

**31** But Paul said to the centurion and the soldiers, "Unless these men stay in the ship, you cannot be saved."

**32** Then the soldiers cut the ropes that held the boat and let it drift away.

**33** Just before daybreak, Paul urged them all to take some food. "Today is the fourteenth day," he said, "that you have been waiting in suspense and going without food, eating nothing at all.

**34** So I urge you to take some food. It will help you to survive, for not a hair from the head of any of you will be lost."[^27]

**35** When he had said this, he took bread, gave thanks to God in front of them all, broke it and began to eat.

**36** They were all encouraged and took some food themselves.

**37** There were 276[^28] of us on the ship in all.

**38** When they had eaten as much as they wanted, they lightened the ship by throwing the wheat[^29] into the sea.

**39** When day came, they did not recognize the land, but they noticed a bay with a beach, and they planned to run the ship ashore there if they could.

**40** So they cut loose the anchors and left them in the sea, and at the same time they loosened the ropes that tied up the steering oars.[^30] Then they hoisted the foresail[^31] to the wind and made for the beach.

**41** But they struck a place where two seas met,[^32] and ran the ship aground. The bow stuck fast and could not be moved, and the stern was being broken up by the pounding.[^33]

**42** The soldiers planned to kill the prisoners, so that none of them would swim away and escape.

**43** But the centurion wanted to save Paul, so he kept them from carrying out their plan. He ordered those who could swim to jump overboard first and get to land,

**44** and the rest to follow, some on planks and some on pieces of the ship.[^34] And so it was that everyone was brought safely to land.

[^1]: Here the narrative again says "we" (see 16:10), and does so until 28:16.
[^2]: A Roman officer in command of about a hundred soldiers; also in verses 6, 11, 31 and 43.
[^3]: Greek *speira Sebaste*, a Roman military unit bearing the honorary title *Augusta*, "the Emperor's". Which unit is meant is uncertain.
[^4]: A port on the northwest coast of Asia Minor, southeast of Troas.
[^5]: That is, along the side of the island sheltered from the wind, probably its eastern and northern coasts, since the winds were from the west.
[^6]: Probably one of the grain ships that carried wheat from Egypt to Rome (see verse 38).
[^7]: Or "would not let us put in there".
[^8]: A cape at the eastern end of Crete.
[^9]: The Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:29–31; 23:26–32), in late September or early October. Sailing on the open Mediterranean was thought dangerous from mid-September, and it stopped altogether in the winter months.
[^10]: Greek *kybernetes*, "helmsman, pilot", the master who steered and commanded the ship, and *naukleros*, the shipowner, or a captain who sailed the ship for its owner.
[^11]: Literally "looking toward Lips and toward Choros", the names of the southwest and northwest winds. Some take this to mean that the harbor looked in the directions toward which those winds blow, that is, northeast and southeast.
[^12]: Greek *asson*, "nearer". Some early translators took it as the name of a place, Assos.
[^13]: Greek *Eurakylon*, a word made from Greek *euros*, "east wind", and Latin *aquilo*, "north wind". Many manuscripts read *Euroklydon*, the reading of the King James Version ("Euroclydon").
[^14]: Literally "rushed down from it", probably from the mountains of Crete.
[^15]: Literally "look the wind in the eye".
[^16]: Many manuscripts read "Clauda".
[^17]: Greek *skaphe*, the small boat usually towed behind a ship; also in verses 30 and 32.
[^18]: Greek *boetheiai*, "helps": probably cables passed around the hull to hold its timbers together. The method is uncertain.
[^19]: The Syrtis, shallow gulfs with shifting sandbanks off the coast of Libya, much feared by sailors.
[^20]: Literally "lowered the gear": perhaps a sea anchor or drag let down to slow the ship, or perhaps the mainsail or the rigging. The meaning is uncertain.
[^21]: Greek *ekbole*, the technical term for throwing cargo overboard to lighten a ship.
[^22]: Greek *skeue*, "equipment", perhaps spare rigging and tackle, or the main yard.
[^23]: Many manuscripts read "we threw".
[^24]: Literally "and gained this disaster and loss", an idiom meaning "and avoided it".
[^25]: Greek *Adrias*, which then meant the whole central Mediterranean between Greece, Italy and Sicily, not only the modern Adriatic.
[^26]: Greek *orguia*, the span of a man's outstretched arms, about 6 feet (1.8 m). Twenty fathoms is about 120 feet (37 m), and fifteen fathoms about 90 feet (27 m).
[^27]: A proverbial expression. Compare 1 Samuel 14:45; Luke 21:18.
[^28]: Literally "276 souls". Some manuscripts read "about seventy-six".
[^29]: Probably the ship's cargo of grain from Egypt (verse 6).
[^30]: Ancient ships were steered by two large oars at the stern. They had been lashed up out of the water while the ship lay at anchor.
[^31]: Greek *artemon*, probably a small sail at the bow. The meaning is not certain.
[^32]: Greek *dithalassos*, "between two seas": perhaps a sandbar or reef with water on both sides, or a channel between an island and the shore. The meaning is uncertain.
[^33]: Literally "by the force". Many manuscripts read "by the force of the waves".
[^34]: Or "on some of the people from the ship", that is, helped by the crew.
