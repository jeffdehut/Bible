**1** They traveled through Amphipolis and Apollonia and came to Thessalonica, where there was a synagogue of the Jews.

**2** As was his custom, Paul went in to them, and on three Sabbaths[^1] he reasoned with them from the Scriptures,

**3** explaining and showing that the Messiah had to suffer and rise from the dead, and saying, "This Jesus, whom I am proclaiming to you, is the Messiah."

**4** Some of them were persuaded and joined Paul and Silas, as did a large number of the Greeks who worshiped God[^2] and not a few of the leading women.

**5** But the Jews became jealous.[^3] They took some wicked men from the marketplace, formed a mob and set the city in an uproar. They attacked Jason's house, trying to find them and bring them out to the people.[^4]

**6** When they did not find them, they dragged Jason and some of the brothers before the city officials,[^5] shouting, "These men who have turned the world upside down have come here too,

**7** and Jason has welcomed them into his house. They are all acting against the decrees of Caesar,[^6] saying that there is another king, Jesus."

**8** The crowd and the city officials who heard this were thrown into turmoil.

**9** After taking security[^7] from Jason and the others, they let them go.

**10** At once, during the night, the brothers and sisters[^8] sent Paul and Silas away to Berea. When they arrived, they went into the synagogue of the Jews.

**11** The people there were more noble-minded than those in Thessalonica. They received the word with great eagerness, examining the Scriptures every day to see whether these things were so.

**12** Many of them therefore believed, along with not a few Greek women of high standing, and men as well.

**13** But when the Jews from Thessalonica learned that the word of God had been proclaimed by Paul in Berea too, they came there as well, stirring up the crowds and throwing them into turmoil.

**14** Then the brothers and sisters immediately sent Paul away to go as far as the coast,[^9] but Silas and Timothy stayed there.

**15** Those who escorted Paul brought him as far as Athens. Then they left, with instructions for Silas and Timothy to come to him as quickly as possible.

**16** While Paul was waiting for them in Athens, his spirit was deeply troubled within him to see that the city was full of idols.

**17** So he reasoned in the synagogue with the Jews and the worshipers of God, and in the marketplace every day with whoever happened to be there.

**18** Some of the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers[^10] also debated with him. Some said, "What is this scrap-picker[^11] trying to say?" Others said, "He seems to be a proclaimer of foreign gods."[^12] They said this because he was proclaiming the good news about Jesus and the resurrection.[^13]

**19** They took him and brought him to the Areopagus,[^14] saying, "May we know what this new teaching is that you are presenting?

**20** For you are bringing some strange things to our ears, and we want to know what they mean."

**21** All the Athenians and the foreigners who lived there spent their time doing nothing but telling or hearing the latest thing.

**22** Then Paul stood in the middle of the Areopagus and said, "Men of Athens, I see that in every way you are very religious.[^15]

**23** For as I walked around and looked carefully at the objects of your worship, I even found an altar with this inscription: 'To an unknown god.' So what you worship without knowing it, this I proclaim to you.[^16]

**24** The God who made the world and everything in it, since he is Lord of heaven and earth, does not live in temples made by human hands.[^17]

**25** Nor is he served by human hands, as if he needed anything, since he himself gives everyone life and breath and everything.

**26** From one man[^18] he made every nation of humankind to live on the whole face of the earth, and he fixed the times appointed for them[^19] and the boundaries of the places where they would live,[^20]

**27** so that they would seek God,[^21] in the hope that they might feel their way toward him and find him. Yet he is not far from each one of us.

**28** For in him we live and move and have our being,[^22] as even some of your own poets have said: 'For we too are his offspring.'[^23]

**29** Since, then, we are God's offspring, we ought not to think that the divine being is like gold or silver or stone, an image shaped by human skill and imagination.

**30** God overlooked the times of ignorance, but now he commands[^24] all people everywhere to repent,

**31** because he has set a day on which he is going to judge the world in righteousness[^25] by a man whom he has appointed. He has given proof[^26] of this to everyone by raising him from the dead."

**32** When they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some began to mock, but others said, "We will hear you again about this."

**33** So Paul went out from among them.

**34** But some men joined him and believed. Among them were Dionysius the Areopagite,[^27] a woman named Damaris, and others with them.

[^1]: Or "for three weeks".
[^2]: That is, Gentiles who worshiped the God of Israel without becoming Jews; also in verse 17.
[^3]: Many manuscripts read "But the Jews who did not believe took …", without "became jealous".
[^4]: Greek *demos*, "the people", perhaps the assembly of the city's citizens.
[^5]: Greek *politarchai*, "politarchs", the title of the chief magistrates of Thessalonica, known from inscriptions there; also in verse 8.
[^6]: The Roman emperor, at this time Claudius (AD 41–54; see 18:2).
[^7]: Literally "taking what was sufficient", a legal expression for a bond or pledge that they would keep the peace.
[^8]: Greek *adelphoi*, "brothers", used of the believers, men and women alike; also in verse 14.
[^9]: Literally "as far as the sea". Many manuscripts read "as if to the sea".
[^10]: The two leading schools of philosophy at the time. Epicureans taught that the gods take no part in human affairs and that the aim of life is a calm and untroubled happiness. Stoics taught that a divine reason runs through all things and that the aim of life is to live in harmony with it.
[^11]: Greek *spermologos*, "seed-picker", a bird that picks up seeds; used of a person who picks up scraps of learning and passes them off as his own.
[^12]: Greek *daimonia*, "divine beings, deities".
[^13]: Greek *anastasis*. Because they speak of "gods", some of the hearers may have taken *Anastasis*, "Resurrection", to be the name of a goddess beside Jesus.
[^14]: The "Hill of Ares", northwest of the Acropolis, and the name of the ancient council of Athens that took its name from the hill. Here and in verse 22 either the place or the council may be meant.
[^15]: Or "very superstitious". The Greek word can be used in praise or in blame.
[^16]: Many manuscripts read "So the one whom you worship without knowing him, this one I proclaim to you."
[^17]: Compare 1 Kings 8:27; Isaiah 42:5; 66:1–2; Acts 7:48–50.
[^18]: Literally "From one". Many manuscripts read "From one blood".
[^19]: Or "the seasons he appointed".
[^20]: Compare Deuteronomy 32:8.
[^21]: Many manuscripts read "the Lord".
[^22]: These words may also come from a Greek poet. A much later tradition attributes them to Epimenides of Crete (about 600 BC).
[^23]: From the poem *Phaenomena* (line 5) by Aratus (3rd century BC), who came from Cilicia, Paul's home province. The Stoic Cleanthes has a similar line in his Hymn to Zeus. In both poems "his" refers to Zeus.
[^24]: Some manuscripts read "he announces to".
[^25]: The words echo Psalm 9:9 (9:8 in most English Bibles), 96:13 and 98:9.
[^26]: Greek *pistis*, which usually means "faith" but here "proof, assurance".
[^27]: That is, a member of the council of the Areopagus (see verse 19).
